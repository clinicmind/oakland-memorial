## What it is

Cross-cultural assessment means collecting test data from people who differ culturally [N-T3-10].

Test adaptation is the work of moving a test from one language and culture to another. The International Test Commission (ITC) separates adaptation from translation. Translation is only one part of adaptation [N-T3-2].

The ITC lists the activities that adaptation covers. They include deciding whether the test can measure the same construct in the second language and culture, selecting translators, and choosing a design to check the translators' work, such as forward and backward translation. They also include modifying the test format, checking the equivalence of the new version, and running other validity studies [N-T3-13].

## Why it matters

Translation alone is a very simplistic approach. It can move words and still ignore educational or psychological equivalence [N-T3-2].

The problem is real in practice. Oakland and colleagues note that many tests were built in Western cultures and shaped by behaviors common there. People still use them in other places, yet little data examines whether they transport across nations [N-T3-5].

Immigrants and second-language learners raise the same issue. Oakland (2016) names five topics that can affect their assessment: qualities of culture and language, the personal qualities assessed, the psychometric properties of tests, the relationship between examinee and examiner, and ethical issues [N-T3-9].

Tests also travel widely. A survey that Oakland and colleagues report gathered data from 64 countries on the standardized tests school psychologists use with children and youth [N-T3-11].

## Professor Oakland's contribution

Oakland served as president of the International Test Commission (see the [UF College of Education statement](https://education.ufl.edu/news/2015/03/09/coe-responds-to-tragic-death-of-professor-emeritus-thomas-oakland/)). The ITC's primary goal, as Oakland describes it, is to help members, affiliates and others exchange information on test development and test use, so that test-related practices improve [N-T3-7].

His writing on the ITC and on international testing includes these works.

1. **ITC history.** Oakland, Poortinga, Schlegel and Hambleton (2001) describe the ITC from its conception by Jean Cardinet in the mid-1960s, through its birth in 1976, to the year 2000 [N-T3-8]. Oakland (2016) wrote a short history of the ITC for the ITC International Handbook [N-T3-7].
2. **International overview.** Oakland (2004) summarizes the history of test development and use, test use internationally, and standards and guidelines. It also discusses regional and international organizations, including the ITC [N-T3-3].
3. **Language and reading.** Oakland and Lane (2004) examine language and reading issues in developing and adapting tests. They discuss the strengths and limits of readability formulas. They say these formulas belong with paragraphs and longer passages, not with single items [N-T3-4].
4. **A worked example.** Oakland, Iliescu, Chen and Chen (2013) describe the Romanian and Taiwan adaptation of a U.S. adaptive behavior measure, the ABAS-II Parent Form for ages 5 to 21. The article describes the steps taken to support a valid translation and cultural adaptation [N-T3-14].
5. **A temperament example.** Benson, Oakland and Shermis (2008) test whether a temperament measure works the same way across countries. See the unit on temperament and learning styles [N-T2-4].

The ITC publishes guidelines on several topics, including one on translating and adapting tests [N-T3-1]. The ITC credits a six-person committee for the second edition of that guideline [N-T3-2]. The diagram shows the six groups of the ITC guidelines for translating and adapting tests [N-T3-2].

- **Translation versus adaptation.** Translation changes the language. Adaptation covers the whole process, including equivalence checks [N-T3-13].
- **Pre-condition decisions.** Decisions come before any translation begins [N-T3-12]. One decision is whether the construct overlaps enough across the groups for the intended use of the scores [N-T3-2].
- **Equivalence.** A good adaptation shows that the new version measures the same thing in the new culture. Oakland and colleagues check this with score differences, internal consistency, intercorrelations and factor structure [N-T3-15].
- **Transportability.** Transportability asks whether a test built in one culture still works in another [N-T3-5].

## In practice

1. **Ask before you adapt.** Decide first whether the test can measure the same construct in the second culture. Do this before any translating [N-T3-12].
2. **Plan the translation check.** Use a design that checks translators' work, for example forward and backward translation [N-T3-13].
3. **Test the new version with data.** The 2013 adaptation study compared three versions on score differences, reliability, intercorrelations and factor structure [N-T3-15].
4. **Read the result with care.** In that study, reliabilities were high and comparable across the three versions, and correlation patterns were sufficiently similar [N-T3-6].
5. **Mind language level.** Readability formulas fit longer passages, not items [N-T3-4].
6. **Think about the examinee.** For immigrants and second-language learners, consider culture, language, acculturation and the relationship with the examiner [N-T3-9].
7. **Use qualified professionals.** A test result depends on the assessor's judgment. This page is not a manual for adapting any particular test.

## Self-check

1. What is the main difference between test translation and test adaptation?

   A. Translation is longer than adaptation.
   B. Translation moves the language. Adaptation covers the whole process, including equivalence checks.
   C. Adaptation never needs data.
   D. Translation is for tests. Adaptation is for questionnaires.

   **Answer:** B. The ITC calls translation only one part of adaptation [N-T3-2].

2. When should a team decide whether a test measures the same construct in the new culture?

   A. After the test goes on sale.
   B. Only after the translation is finished.
   C. Before the translation begins.
   D. Never, if the translators are skilled.

   **Answer:** C. The ITC places this among the pre-condition decisions, which come before translation [N-T3-12].

3. Why do cross-national adaptation studies collect score and factor-structure data?

   A. To prove the original test is wrong.
   B. To check that the adapted versions behave in similar ways.
   C. To remove the need for a manual.
   D. To rank cultures.

   **Answer:** B. Equivalence is an empirical question, so the 2013 study compared score differences, reliability and factor structure [N-T3-5].

4. According to Oakland and Lane (2004), where do readability formulas fit best?

   A. Single test items.
   B. Paragraphs and longer passages.
   C. Every part of a test.
   D. Numbers only.

   **Answer:** B. They advise limiting such formulas to paragraphs and longer passages [N-T3-4].
